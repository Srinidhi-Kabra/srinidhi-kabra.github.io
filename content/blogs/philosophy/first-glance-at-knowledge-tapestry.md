Creativity is one of the most fundamental expressions that humanity has developed, intrinsically. Any form of innovation, imagination or philosophical reflection can be argued to stem from this creativity in an attempt to 'understand' (whatever the verb may mean) the world we inhabit. This creativity could be thought of as being led by a delicate balance between two forces - curiosity and hope - something quite innate to humans. It is worth emphasising the fragility of the interaction of these forces. Curiosity without any hope collapses into nihilism, after all, to understand is to hope one could. Whereas, hope without curiosity collapses into dogma, something that is all too familiar to anyone with a keen eye for observation along with little understanding of history and culture. In this sense, true understanding - one which mirrors the physical reality, must begin with a diaphanous reconciliation of these two. Modern science is truly unique in the history of ontological exploration in the form through which this balance is maintained, nihilism is avoided while dogma is restrained against. 

It must be noted that while the avenues of exploration such as 'meaning of existence', 'architecture of human mind' or 'understanding of the physical world' may seem the most 'natural' to us; it is far from. Our ambitions are shaped not by some extrinsic metric of worth, rather by our intrinsic perception of the world. In this sense, in the vastness of knowledge, one only explores which one deems as worthy of it. For a long time in history, study of the natural world beyond the religious texts was unfathomable whereas today, it would be an approach looked down upon. Clearly, our ambitions are shaped by our experiences and environment which is ever evolving over time. If we regard as there to be a static space comprised of all possible knowledge, we only explore what we deem worthy. It would be highly narcissistic of us to suggest this space is comprised only of the knowledge we could comprehend or even hope to one day. One cannot dismiss the possibility of there existing knowledge that human ambition would never concern itself with in the future one could foresee, after all, undoubtedly intelligent as Newton and Pascal may have been, could never have been able to comprehend the possibility of this world defying all fathomable intuition at the quantum scale. From this observation one could conclude human knowledge to be a rather small subset of this space which is ever enlarging albeit only in the direction the individual (by extension the society) in question deems worthy. One could envision this rather poetically as the ever evolving grand tapestry of our understanding, shaped only by the dynamic nature of human ambition. 

Thus, the question of what 'understanding a phenomenon' could be translated to locating and examining the specific phenomenon in this grand tapestry, present in the form of, say, a knot. It is thus unquestionably clear that to understand is to trace all possible threads between this knot and all the ones surrounding it. This raises many problems however as the tapestry grows by forming these connections. Additionally, the nature of the knot itself is worth examining. One could then categorise understanding through the following modes,
1. _Local understanding_ : this would represent analysing the structure of the knot itself, from the thread it is composed of, to its shape.
2. _Contextual Understanding_ : analysing and situating the knot in the grand tapestry itself with reference to the knots it is connected to. Note that this contextualisation will certainly involve local understanding of the neighbouring knots for each one's threads intermingle to form the connection observed.
3. _Architecturally Generative Understanding_ : forming new connections with knots previously not observed. 
So far the conversation has been restricted to holistically understanding of a particular knot, which itself is a form of local understanding when concerned with the entire tapestry. One must add _spontaneous generation_ to this list which amounts to the formation of a new knot itself. Although, this cannot be realised as a direct understanding of the phenomenon for one must go through first architecturally generative understanding to establish a connection with the newly formed knot and then contextualise it to understand the phenomenon. Additionally, to understand the tapestry itself it is necessary to understand the connections in their own right instead of in the context of a particular knot. One could call this _categorical understanding_. It is thus clear that 'to understand wholly' is incomplete with just the local picture, where syntactic manipulations would lie. 

Enough has been commented about the epistemological consequences of this analogy for a first glance yet some aspects of the ontology underlying this analogy has not been explored. A keen reader may observe an unusual choice of adjective in the opening paragraph of this essay, where I describe the balance between curiosity and hope as being 'diaphanous' - an adjective usually reserved for translucent, delicate clothing. Beyond poetic beauty, it serves to highlight an important aspect of this interaction. One may be tempted to understand where on the spectrum between paralysing nihilism and suppressive dogma does one's balance between curiosity and hope lie. As tempting as it may be, I would argue it to be a hopeless endeavour. One can attempt to situate oneself on this spectrum, but to do so one already utilises their present position, meaning, a true external perspective is never achieved for it would require one to step outside of their environmental conditioning which we can safely regard as being impossible. This in itself, does not grant the absence of any vision whatsoever when it comes to the primary question. However, it does highlight the opacity without lucidity that one experiences in an attempt to situate themselves along the spectrum. 

An objection could be raised where two temporally distant points could be selected with enormous differences of cultural ontology, would these not effectively count as external perspectives with respect to one another? To this, I would point out that if these two were cultures were truly independent of one another, perhaps they would serve as a close enough approximation as long as both the narratives are considered complementarily, for otherwise the previous objection still stands. But historically, any generation is influenced by the culture of the previous one as such, the cultural ontology of one of these cultures would be shaped entirely, albeit indirectly, by the other one. This observation clearly resolves the objection. 

In conclusion, I would argue for the poetic beauty along with lucidity this analogy offers in articulating the relationship of knowledge with humanity itself.  A popular philosophical view often regards deductive knowledge such as mathematics to be intrinsic to nature itself. This analogy however clearly shows this to be false, for we clearly have much more to say about number theory than say, how to tie shoelaces. Simple reason being, one is clearly more of interest than the other, reflecting ambition as being the shaping force of this tapestry. This further raises questions about the importance of many of these 'knots' themselves, do they carry intrinsic importance or is it simply central to a certain region because the tapestry grew around them? Perhaps this philosophical questions would do much good to keep in mind when attempting to understand for knowledge can only be holistically obtained by contextualising it within all that we know.
